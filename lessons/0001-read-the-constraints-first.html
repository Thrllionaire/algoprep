Read the Constraints First

Before you understand the problem, you can already know what the answer cannot be.

You told me the failure is "I can't pick the pattern." Recognition is hard. Elimination is easy — and it does most of the work. The input constraints tell you your time budget, the budget rules out whole families of technique, and what's left is usually two or three candidates. You can do this in fifteen seconds, on a problem you do not yet understand.

The one idea

Every problem ships a bound on its input. That bound is not flavour text; it is the interviewer telling you the complexity they expect. Work backwards: a machine does very roughly 108 simple operations per second, so if n can be 100,000, then an O(n2) idea needs 1010 operations and is dead — before you have had a single thought about the problem itself.

The 108 figure is the USACO Guide's deliberately conservative estimate; it notes the real number "could be closer to 5·108 given good constant factors." Treat it as an order of magnitude, not a measurement.

So the move is mechanical. Find the largest n. Look up the slowest complexity you can still afford. Everything more expensive than that is eliminated. Try it — type a bound, or tap a preset:

Notice what just happened at n = 109: the budget is so tight you cannot even look at every element. That single observation eliminates every array-scanning pattern you will ever learn. You did not need to understand the problem to know that.

Reading the bound in the other direction

The ladder is more useful read right-to-left. A bound is not just a speed limit; it is a fingerprint. Certain numbers appear only when a specific family is intended:

Tell — n ≤ about 20

A neon sign for exponential. No other family needs a bound that small, and no problem setter picks 18 by accident. 218 is 262,144 — nothing. 230 is a billion — impossible. When you see a bound in the teens, stop looking for a clever polynomial trick and start asking "what am I enumerating — subsets, or orderings?"

Tell — "return the answer modulo 109 + 7"

The answer is a count so astronomically large it would overflow a 64-bit integer. You therefore cannot be producing the things you are counting, only counting them. That is a counting DP, nearly always. Here the modulo does not just make brute force slow, it makes brute force the wrong shape.

Tell — a bound in the low hundreds

Too big for 2n, far too small to need n log n. That gap is deliberate: it is buying you a cubic algorithm. In interview problems that almost always means interval DP — a dp[i][j] over a range, with an inner loop picking a split point k. The third loop is what the loose bound pays for.

Two more worth memorising, both on the reference card: two separate sequences each bounded around 103 multiplies out to a 106-cell table, which is the signature of 2-D DP over a pair (edit distance, LCS). And values bounded tightly while n is huge (0 ≤ nums[i] ≤ 100) invites counting over the value range instead of the index range.

Practice

Six problems. You get the constraints and one line of description — deliberately not enough to solve anything. Your job is only to name the budget and the surviving family. That is the whole skill.

Saying it out loud

You are interviewing at Staff+, where the reasoning is the deliverable and the code is evidence. So do not do this silently. Thirty seconds of narration, before you write anything:

"Before I dig in — n goes up to 10^5, so I'm targeting n log n
or better. That rules out comparing all pairs. I also notice the
subarray has to be contiguous and the values are all positive,
which means a window can only grow and shrink monotonically. So
I'd like to try a two-pointer window for O(n). Let me check that
against an example before I commit."

That paragraph does four things a strong interviewer is explicitly listening for: it establishes a budget, eliminates an approach with a reason, names the property of the input that licenses the chosen technique, and proposes verification before committing. Note the last sentence especially — announcing that you will check before you commit is what separates confident from reckless.

Two honest limits. First, this narrows the field; it does not pick the winner. At O(n) you must still choose between sliding window, prefix sum, and a monotonic stack — that is the next lesson's job. Second, a human interviewer may give you no constraints at all. Then ask: "roughly how large can n get?" That question reads as seniority, and the answer collapses your search space exactly as above.
