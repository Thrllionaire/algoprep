A short course

Algorithm Intuition

Looking at an unfamiliar problem and knowing, in the first two minutes, which family of techniques it belongs to — and why.

Most interview prep teaches you patterns one at a time and hopes recognition emerges. This course runs the other way: it teaches triage first. Ruling out the techniques that cannot apply is far easier than naming the one that does, and it does most of the work.

Lessons

  1. Read the Constraints First The input bound tells you your time budget, and the budget eliminates whole families of technique — before you understand the problem. Interactive constraint ladder plus a six-question triage drill.
  2. The Shape of the Answer Count the candidates the answer's shape implies, divide by the budget, and the machinery is forced. Includes the one contrast that fails silently if you get it wrong: sliding window versus prefix sums.
  3. The Window Invariant Write one sentence before the loop and the shrink condition stops being something you recall. Includes a step-through trace where you place left yourself, and the longest-versus-shortest inversion that catches almost everyone.
  4. The Prefix Pair Two CSES problems, one word apart, and the technique changes completely. Stop thinking about subarrays and think about the boundaries either side of them — then the hash map is just how you stop paying for the search.
  5. The Inward Walk Two Sum, sorted or not: the same pair, one word of difference, and the licence this time is sortedness rather than a monotonic predicate — walking in from both ends instead of sweeping in one direction.
  6. Choosing the Search Space Binary search on the answer — the one case where you build the search space out of a range of candidates instead of being handed one, licensed by a monotonic feasibility check instead of a sorted array.
  7. Recursion, Memo, Table The same coin-problem function, written three ways, with the call count dropping from 4.7 million to 91 between the naive and memoized versions — recursion, memoization and tabulation as one continuous idea rather than three topics.
  8. What the Children Return Trees and DFS, where the recursion is the data structure — so the pattern is free and the return value is the whole decision. Includes the diameter bug that is wrong on 81% of random trees and right on every tree you would sketch by hand.
  9. The Root Knows One Thing Heaps: the first licence in this course that's a partial order rather than a full one. A min-heap capped at k is the classic inversion for “k-th largest,” and forgetting the cap is wrong on 99% of queries after the first.
  10. Which End You Sort By Two interval problems, same input shape, opposite sort key: keep the most (sort by end) versus merge into coverage (sort by start). Swap the key on either one and it's wrong on about one interval set in six.
  11. Counting Maximum Overlap Meeting Rooms II, solved two ways: a heap of end times, and a sweep-line counter over arrival/departure events. Getting the tie-break wrong — arrivals before departures — is wrong on 36% of trials and invisible on every input with no touching endpoints.
  12. Bitmask and Backtracking Coming next. The family lesson 0001's n ≤ 20 tell has been pointing at since the first lesson.

Reference

  1. The Constraint Ladder Bound → budget → surviving families, plus the eight tells, on one printable page. Built to reread; the lessons are scaffolding.
  2. Shape to Family Candidate counts per shape, the full shape × return-type matrix, the window-vs-prefix-sum rule, and both Python templates.
  3. Window Invariants The skeleton's four blanks, all three update positions, the longest and shortest templates side by side, and the table of cases where a window is the wrong tool.
  4. The Prefix Pair The identity, the four templates, the key-selection table, the four silent bugs, and the window-versus-prefix decision on one printable page.
  5. Tree Recursion The two questions to ask before writing a tree function, the three skeletons, which one each classic problem needs, the four silent bugs, and the recursion-depth fix.
